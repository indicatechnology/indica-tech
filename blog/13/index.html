The £80K question — why construction software is about to get cheap on purpose

A Tier 2 main contractor in the North West opens the renewal invoice for their construction management stack last week. The headline number reads £80,000 for the year — Procore at the centre, Aconex bolted on for the document control workflow, two named customer-success contacts, an implementation services line for the Building Safety Act submission template the contractor needed last spring. The finance director queries the line items and the answer that comes back is the answer the whole industry is about to stop accepting: roughly four-fifths of that invoice is not software. It is the implementation labour, the configuration consultancy, the bespoke training, the customisation engineers and the account management hours required to make a generic platform behave like a regulated workflow. AI-native vertical platforms have spent 2025 quietly stripping that services layer out. The same Golden Thread record under the Building Safety Act 2022 now stands up in a week, at around £3,500 per high-risk building project, on a voice-driven AI-native tool with no implementation invoice attached. That is not a discount. It is the absence of a services tax that AI does not need to charge. The CFO who signs the £80K renewal this quarter is not buying software. They are buying the legacy stack’s payroll, six months before their competitor stops buying it altogether.

Executive summary

The price of vertical SaaS in regulated construction is structurally inflated, not accidentally so. A generic platform like Procore or Oracle Aconex is built to serve every customer and so fits no specific workflow out of the box; the gap between the product and the regulated process is closed by paid implementation, paid configuration, paid training, paid customisation, and paid account-management labour. For a Tier 2 UK main contractor running Building Safety Act submissions, this services tax is roughly £56,000 of an £80,000 annual invoice. Andreessen Horowitz’s “services-as-software” thesis — that AI is unbundling the human labour wrapper around software, not the software itself — explains why an AI-native vertical platform can run the same compliant workflow at roughly 1/22nd of the stack cost without being any cheaper to build. The savings are not a discount; they are the absence of the services tax. The catch — and the reason most incumbents are not yet panicking — is that most AI-native vertical challengers are still at demo maturity, not production. Anthropic’s published agent-reliability research puts the long-horizon production threshold for autonomous workflows at around 95%; most demo-stage tools clear 70% in the controlled walkthrough and collapse below 60% on a real site. The teardown that follows is the BSA Golden Thread comparison — legacy stack at £15,000 and twelve weeks per high-risk building project, AI-native at roughly £3,500 and one week — the demo-to-production catch that separates the real challenger from the slide deck, the six questions every construction CFO and CTO should ask any vendor this quarter, and the 2028 prediction the procurement plan should be built around now.

You are not paying for software you cannot configure. You are paying for the human labour required to make generic software fit a regulated workflow. AI just made that labour optional. The price falls follow.

The £80,000 teardown

The invoice almost no construction CFO has ever asked their finance team to itemise looks roughly like this on a Tier 2 main contractor running Procore as the spine of project controls, Aconex on the side for document control, and a Building Safety Act submission template that was custom-built last spring to clear Gateway 2. Licence circa £24,000 (30%): the actual software product, named-user or per-project pricing, the line item that would survive on any modern SaaS contract. Implementation circa £24,000 (30%): the partner-channel professional services hours required to stand the platform up against the contractor’s actual project structure, work breakdown, cost code framework, and regulatory schemas. Training circa £16,000 (20%): the on-site and remote hours required to take site engineers, document controllers, and project managers from never having seen the tool to producing audit-grade records. Customisation circa £8,000 (10%): the bespoke development to make Procore’s default schemas emit a Golden Thread that Building Safety Regulator inspectors will accept. Support circa £8,000 (10%): named account management, escalation paths and bug fixes during the year. Add it up and the software itself is 30% of the invoice. The remaining 70% is human labour reshaping a generic tool into something specific. That ratio is not a Procore problem; it is a vertical SaaS problem. The same shape appears on every Oracle Aconex, Bentley SYNCHRO, Trimble Viewpoint and Asite invoice we have torn down in the last twelve months.

Why this price is structural, not accidental

The vertical SaaS pricing model that produced this invoice was rational under pre-AI economics. A platform sold into thirty regulated sub-industries cannot ship a UI that fits each one out of the box; the marginal cost of configuration would crush margins. So the platform ships a configurable kernel, accepts that configuration is the customer’s problem, and either employs the consultants to do the configuration in-house or licenses the work to a partner channel. Configuration is then priced by the day at the prevailing rate for someone who can hold both a construction workflow and a Procore data model in their head — roughly £600–£1,200 per day in the UK market. A Tier 2 contractor onboarding to a new Procore deployment is a thirty-to-sixty-day engagement before the first compliant record is produced. The implementation invoice and the training invoice are therefore not extracted rents; they are the rational cost of the labour required to translate generic software into a regulated workflow. The pricing model is not predatory. It is a faithful reflection of the underlying production function. What AI changes is the production function. Once a model can hold the workflow and the data model in its own context — and reach for a regulator-shaped schema on demand — the configuration labour stops being a constraint. The pricing model that depended on it stops being defensible.

What AI-native vertical platforms do differently

Three structural moves separate an AI-native vertical platform from a legacy vertical SaaS with an LLM bolted into the help chat. None of them are about model quality.

One: narrow wedge instead of broad kernel. A legacy vertical platform ships a configurable kernel that has to serve every regulated sub-industry. An AI-native challenger picks a single regulated workflow — Golden Thread submissions for high-risk buildings, daily site diary capture for HSE compliance, RAMS approvals for Tier 1 supply chain — and ships a single-purpose tool that is opinionated to the workflow on day one. The configuration burden falls because there is less surface to configure. A16z’s services-as-software thesis names this as the foundational structural move: pick a workflow where most of the cost was human labour, not software, and absorb the labour with AI. The narrow wedge is what makes that absorption tractable.

Two: AI absorbs the configuration layer. Where a legacy platform expects a configuration consultant to write the field mappings, the workflow rules and the regulatory schemas, an AI-native tool expects a model to do it in conversation with the site team. The site manager dictates how the contractor’s project structure differs from the platform’s default and the tool reshapes itself; the document controller speaks the format the Building Safety Regulator inspector wants the Golden Thread record in and the model produces a compliant export. The configuration burden does not disappear; it is absorbed by the model in real time, at roughly £0.05 per inference rather than £800 per consultant-day.

Three: the interface workers already use. The third move is the one most underestimated by procurement teams that have never trialled a vertical AI tool on a live site. Legacy platforms have a desktop UI that assumes the user is at a desk. The site reality is gloves, mud, two minutes between deliveries, a phone in a heavy-duty case. Voice-first capture, with the model reconciling the dictation against the project’s data model in the background, is the interface modality that lands on site. The training invoice collapses because there is nothing to train; the site engineer was already speaking the way the tool expects them to.

The services-as-software economics

The headline cost gap — an £80,000 legacy stack collapsing to roughly £3,500 per high-risk building project on an AI-native equivalent — is a 22x reduction at the stack level and is mathematically inevitable once the services layer is removed. The legacy invoice contains roughly £24,000 of software and £56,000 of human labour wrapped around it. An AI-native vertical platform does not pay the £56,000 in labour because no human ever performs the equivalent work; the inference cost the platform vendor incurs to do the equivalent reshaping is in the low hundreds of pounds per year per customer, and the margin reduction is absorbed by the vendor’s ability to serve more customers per engineering head. The pricing that flows back to the buyer is therefore not a competitive-pressure discount that the legacy vendor can match by cutting margin. It is a structural absence. Gartner’s 2026 vertical SaaS forecast indicates that approximately 35% of regulated vertical SaaS spend will be displaced or repriced under AI-native pressure by 2028; the firms surveyed who model the displacement at the stack level rather than the per-seat level land in roughly the same range. The number that matters is not the discount; it is the ratio. A 22x stack ratio cannot be closed with a 30% concession at renewal. The legacy invoice has a floor — the cost of paying humans to configure software — that the AI-native invoice does not have.

The teardown — Procore plus Aconex vs an AI-native vertical on the BSA Golden Thread

The Building Safety Act 2022 introduced a statutory duty on dutyholders of high-risk buildings to maintain a Golden Thread of information — an audit-grade, structured record of the design, construction and operational decisions made on the building, transferable between parties on demand. The Building Safety Regulator’s enforcement statistics through 2025 confirm the regime has teeth: 28 enforcement notices issued in the first eighteen months of operation, 11 of them tied directly to deficient or non-transferable Golden Thread records. The schema is statutory; the cost of getting it wrong is personal liability up to two years’ imprisonment for mismanaged transfer. This is the cleanest available test case for the legacy-versus-AI-native comparison because the outcome is binary: the inspector either accepts the record or does not.

On the legacy stack, the workflow runs through Procore for project controls, Aconex for document transmittals, a bespoke Golden Thread template configured during a twelve-week implementation, and one document controller trained on each project to maintain the record. Time to first compliant record: twelve weeks. Configuration hours: roughly one hundred and forty. Cost per high-risk building project: approximately £15,000 once the implementation, training and customisation are amortised across the relevant portfolio of HRB projects per year. On the AI-native side, the same workflow runs through a voice-first capture tool that the site team uses on a phone; the model reconciles the dictation against a Golden Thread schema the vendor maintains centrally; the document controller approves rather than authors; the Gateway 2 export is a one-click handover that the inspector accepts in the same form. Time to first compliant record: one week. Configuration hours: roughly six. Cost per high-risk building project: approximately £3,500. The compliant outcome is identical. The ratio is 4x cheaper, 12x faster, with a configuration burden roughly twenty-three times lower per project. Scaled across the Tier 2 contractor’s annual HRB portfolio, the legacy £80,000 stack-level cost collapses to roughly £3,500 to £7,000 of AI-native spend — the 22x figure at the stack level. From the buyer’s side, the line item that used to be “construction management platform” on the operating plan becomes the rounding error.

The demo-to-production catch

The reason most legacy incumbents are not yet panicking — and the reason the buyer cannot simply rip out Procore tomorrow — is that the catch in the AI-native pitch is real and is exactly the catch the rest of this blog has been written about for the last two years. Most AI tools that demo well do not survive contact with a live construction site. The demo lives in a controlled, well-lit office with a single test record on a charged tablet, on a stable network, while the founder enunciates clearly into a clip-on microphone. The production reality is mud, rain, gloves, two minutes between deliveries, intermittent 4G, a site engineer with a strong regional accent, and a Building Safety Regulator inspector who will reject the export if any of the seventeen statutory fields are missing.

Anthropic’s published agent-reliability research, summarised in their 2025–2026 long-horizon evaluation work, puts the reliability threshold at which an autonomous workflow is safe to run in production at approximately 95% step-completion on the relevant task. Most demo-stage construction AI tools clear roughly 70% on a controlled walkthrough and collapse below 60% on real-site capture. That is not a marketing gap to close with a roadmap slide. It is a production gap to close with months of evaluation and hardening work that most early-stage AI vendors have not yet done. The buyer who switches stack on the demo alone is the buyer who has to switch back six months later because the inspector rejected the export. From an architectural angle, the missing layers are the same five that every production AI system needs: a documented evaluation harness against representative site data, a fallback path when the model is uncertain, a kill switch when the workflow stalls, structured logging the regulator can audit, and a human approval gate on irreversible operations. Most AI-native construction tools in 2026 have one or two of those five. None of the ones we have audited have all five. The 95% threshold is what separates an “impressive demo” from a defensible production deployment. The legacy incumbents are buying time on the gap. The challengers that close the gap first are the ones that take 35% of regulated vertical SaaS spend by 2028.

Six questions for the next renewal meeting

The six questions below are designed to expose both legacy bloat and demo-stage AI weakness from the same interview. Use them on the Procore account manager who shows up to defend the renewal and on the AI-native founder who shows up to displace it. The first three flush out legacy services tax; the last three flush out unproductionised AI.

One — What percentage of last year’s invoice was software licence and what percentage was services labour? Show me the line items. A legacy vendor who cannot show the split, or shows it and the services share is over 50%, is selling you the configuration consultancy with a software wrapper. The CFO question. Also the CTO question, because the same split tells you how much of your platform spend is buying capability versus buying configuration that ought to be absorbed by the tool.

Two — What is the time-to-first-compliant-record for a new HRB project, measured in calendar weeks from purchase order to inspector-accepted export? Procore plus Aconex on a typical Tier 2 deployment is twelve weeks. AI-native challengers that cannot beat four weeks have not yet absorbed the services layer. Anything quoting under two weeks is either AI-native or untruthful.

Three — What is the configuration burden in engineer-days per year and who pays for those days? If the answer involves a partner-channel professional services line on top of the licence, you are still on the services-as-software side of the unbundling line, not the other side.

Four — What is your published reliability rate on a representative production sample, measured against the 95% step-completion threshold Anthropic and the rest of the agent-reliability research community now use? An AI-native vendor that cannot answer this in a number does not have an evaluation harness. An evaluation harness is the prerequisite for production. No harness, no production.

Five — What happens when the model is uncertain? Show me the fallback ladder, the kill switch, and the human approval gate on the export to the regulator. The CTO question. If the answer is “the user just edits the record afterwards,” the tool is a demo, not a production system.

Six — What does the audit trail look like when the Building Safety Regulator asks who or what authored a specific field on the Golden Thread? Structured logging under a named identity, with the model and version, the prompt context, and the human approver attributed per field, is the threshold for defensibility. Anything less and the personal-liability exposure under the Building Safety Act sits squarely on the dutyholder.

The 2028 prediction

By 2028, every regulated construction sub-vertical will have at least one AI-native challenger at 10–30% of incumbent stack-level price. The Golden Thread workflow is the cleanest example because the regulator’s acceptance is binary, but the same teardown applies to RAMS approvals, HSE site diary capture, CDM coordinator handovers, quality and snagging registers, defect liability period management, and the entire envelope of MEP commissioning evidence. Each one is currently priced with a 50–70% services tax for the same structural reason: the workflow is regulated and specific, the platform is generic and configurable, and a human pair of hands is required to close the gap. Each one is absorbable by an AI-native vertical wedge that picks the workflow, ships an opinionated tool, and prices the offering at the inference cost of the configuration plus a defensible margin. Gartner’s 2026 vertical SaaS displacement forecast lands on roughly 35% of regulated vertical SaaS spend repricing or displacing under AI-native pressure by 2028, which is broadly the range that falls out of any honest stack-level teardown. The procurement implication for any construction CFO with a multi-year construction software commitment is unambiguous: the renewal clauses written in 2024 and 2025 did not anticipate this displacement; the renewal clauses written in 2026 and 2027 must include explicit exit paths, data portability commitments, and a no-penalty rip-and-replace window keyed to the moment a regulator-defensible AI-native equivalent emerges in the relevant sub-vertical. The legacy incumbent that survives 2028 is the legacy incumbent that does the same unbundling work on themselves first — ships an AI-native module priced without the services tax, accepts the margin compression, and uses the install base as the moat. Most will not.

Risks and what to avoid

Don’t switch stack on a demo alone. The demo is the controlled walkthrough; the production reality is the site. Run a parallel pilot against one live HRB project, measure the inspector-acceptance rate, and only switch on the back of that number, not on the founder’s deck.

Don’t mistake LLM features bolted into Procore for AI-native unbundling. An AI help chat inside a legacy product does not remove the implementation invoice. The unbundling lives in the workflow architecture, not in the chat box.

Don’t sign a multi-year legacy renewal without an AI-displacement clause. The renewal terms negotiated in 2026 will outlast the procurement assumptions that produced them. The exit path, the data portability commitment, and the no-penalty replacement window are the three clauses every construction CFO needs in writing this quarter.

Don’t leave the CTO out of the conversation. The 22x figure is a CFO line on the operating plan, but the BSA Golden Thread defensibility is a CTO line in the architecture review. The decision to switch stack has to clear both desks. The vendor that cannot answer either set of questions cleanly is not the right vendor.

What good looks like — one year from now

The construction software line on the operating plan is no longer the largest line outside payroll. The Tier 2 main contractor runs an AI-native vertical platform on one or two of the regulated workflows where the AI maturity has reached the 95% step-completion threshold, and runs the legacy stack on the workflows where it has not, with a written switching plan against each. The renewal contracts signed since the start of 2026 include AI-displacement clauses, data portability commitments and no-penalty exit windows. Every Building Safety Regulator export passes through a documented fallback ladder, a kill switch and a human approval gate; the audit trail attributes every field to a model, a version, and a human approver, with structured logging under named identities. The board can answer, in writing, the question “what is our cost-per-high-risk-building-project on the Golden Thread workflow, on each of our active platforms,” in under five minutes. Most boards in UK construction cannot today. The ones that can are the ones that will not be paying £80,000 a year for the services tax this time next year.

Final thought

The vertical SaaS pricing model that produced the £80,000 invoice was rational under pre-AI economics and is structurally indefensible under post-AI economics. The legacy incumbents will dress the next renewal as a competitive concession, a roadmap addition, an AI feature bolted into the existing product. None of that closes a 22x stack-level ratio. The buyer who treats the renewal as a procurement event misses the actual question, which is architectural. The buyer who treats it as an architectural event — rebuilds the workflow around the absence of the services tax, accepts the parallel-pilot risk, and writes the displacement clauses into every contract signed this year — is the buyer who will not feature in the 2028 case study about how Tier 2 construction overpaid for a decade. Demo to production. That’s the gap, that’s the wedge, that’s the entire game.
